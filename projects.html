Semiconductor RTL Design Intensive Training Program

Completed

+

Jun 2026 – Jul 2026

1. Introduction

This was a mission-based intensive RTL and FPGA design training program focused on developing fundamental digital design and SoC implementation skills.

For each mission, only the required specifications, interface requirements, and relevant datasheets were provided. I independently developed the RTL architecture from the initial design concept, implemented the design in Verilog, and verified it on FPGA hardware. Because the designs were developed independently without pre-built RTL solutions, the process involved substantial hands-on trial and error, allowing me to encounter and resolve practical design and debugging issues firsthand.

The missions progressed from fundamental RTL and finite-state-machine design to peripheral communication, AMBA bus protocols, memory interfaces, and Zynq PS–PL integration, providing a practical foundation in FPGA-based SoC design.

  • Verilog RTL Design
  • Finite State Machine (FSM) Design
  • FPGA Synthesis and Implementation
  • SPI MASTER Peripheral Controller Design
  • AMBA APB3 Protocol and Custom Slave IP
  • AXI-Based Zynq PS–PL Integration
  • Block RAM (BRAM) Interface Design
  • Vivado ILA Hardware Debugging
  • Bare-Metal C with Vitis
  • Datasheet-Driven Hardware Development

3. Mission I — FPGA RTL Fundamentals

Four-Way Traffic Light Controller

Designed a four-way traffic-light controller using a finite state machine, including left-turn vehicle signals and pedestrian signals. Implemented clock division, RTL control logic, XDC pin constraints, FPGA bitstream generation, and on-board verification.

Technical Challenges & Debugging

  • Minor debugging was required when merging four one-way traffic-light FSM modules into one top module, including internal wiring and proper wire-type declarations.
  • After creating the first HDL draft, I realized that the design was operating directly from the system clock rather than using a one-second timing period. I therefore scaled the clock to a one-second period and implemented LED flickering using an additional registered timing signal.
  • Since this was my first mission involving board implementation, I initially struggled to find the correct XDC reference information and match the FPGA pins. It took a substantial amount of time to extract the necessary information from the datasheets and board schematics.

10. Training Reflection

A substantial amount of inference and analysis was required because I did not have a strong background in RTL or FPGA design when I began this training. Through the progression of the missions, I became more confident in my ability to learn unfamiliar technical concepts and translate hardware specifications and peripheral datasheets into RTL architectures and working Verilog implementations.

The project-based process also taught me that perfection is not always the best engineering strategy. Spending too much time trying to make an initial design perfect can leave less time and energy for implementation and debugging. I therefore learned to approach initial designs more directly, verify them incrementally, and improve them through debugging.

This training also gave me a broader perspective on electrical engineering and semiconductor systems. My previous interests were more focused on analog semiconductor devices, but working with FPGA-based digital systems helped me better understand how interfaces, controllers, memories, processing systems, and other hardware blocks interact within a complete system.

11. Training Outcome

Through these missions, I became much more familiar with register-based systems, including how registers work with addresses and how different I/O protocols communicate with peripherals.

Without relying mainly on textbooks or lectures, I learned many of these concepts directly through the projects by searching how each protocol works and applying the specifications to my own designs. This helped me build a more practical understanding of computer architecture and how different hardware blocks communicate with each other.

I also became more comfortable working with datasheets. As I worked with new devices and protocols, I learned how to find timing requirements, register information, initialization sequences, and other specifications from unfamiliar technical documents and use them in actual RTL implementations.

Overall, the training lowered the barrier to approaching unfamiliar hardware, protocols, and technical documentation, while giving me a practical foundation for more complex FPGA-based SoC and hardware-accelerator projects.

MOSFET Physics and SONOS Memory Analysis using Sentaurus TCAD

Completed

+

May 2026

1. Introduction

This project focuses on understanding Synopsys Sentaurus TCAD simulation. The main objective was to simulate semiconductor devices in a virtual environment and visualize their electrical and physical behavior by adjusting device parameters and command code. Through numerical simulation, MOSFET operation, short-channel effects (SCE), device optimization techniques, and SONOS memory operation were investigated. This project connected theoretical semiconductor concepts with practical device simulation and visualization.

2. TCAD Workflow

Sentaurus Workbench was used to organize the simulation flow. Device structures were created in Sentaurus Structure Editor (SDE), electrical characteristics were simulated in Sentaurus Device (SDevice), and physical quantities were visualized in Sentaurus Visual.

3. Device Construction

Doping Structure

The nMOS device structure was generated using Sentaurus Structure Editor (SDE). The source and drain regions were heavily doped, while the substrate remained lightly doped to form p-n junctions.

Mesh Structure

Mesh refinement was applied near the gate oxide and source/drain junctions, where strong electric-field gradients occur. Fine meshing improves numerical accuracy in regions with rapid physical variation.

4. MOSFET Fundamentals

Output Characteristics

The drain current increases linearly at low drain voltage and gradually enters saturation as the channel approaches pinch-off. Higher gate voltage produces stronger inversion and increases the drain current.

Transfer Characteristics

The transfer curve shows the transition from cutoff to strong inversion. Current below the threshold voltage follows exponential behavior, known as subthreshold conduction.

Long-Channel vs. Short-Channel MOSFET

Compared with the long-channel device, the short-channel MOSFET shows larger off-state leakage and degraded gate control. This behavior is caused by stronger drain-channel coupling.

5. Short-Channel Effects

Threshold Voltage Roll-Off

As channel length decreases, threshold voltage decreases due to charge sharing between the source and drain depletion regions. This effect is known as threshold voltage roll-off.

Subthreshold Swing Degradation

The 50 nm device exhibits a more gradual subthreshold slope and higher leakage current. This degradation results from increased short-channel effects and weaker gate control over the channel.

DIBL

Increasing drain bias lowers the source-channel potential barrier and effectively reduces threshold voltage. This phenomenon is known as Drain-Induced Barrier Lowering (DIBL).

Punch-Through

When the source and drain depletion regions merge, carriers can flow directly between the source and drain without sufficient gate control. This leakage mechanism is known as punch-through current.

6. Short-Channel Improvement

LDD Structure

A lightly doped drain (LDD) extension was introduced between the channel and the heavily doped drain region. The LDD device exhibits lower leakage current and improved subthreshold behavior because the drain electric field is distributed over a larger distance, reducing DIBL and hot-carrier effects.

Halo+LDD vs. LDD Implantation

Halo implantation introduces a locally higher body doping concentration near the source and drain junctions. Compared with the LDD-only structure, the Halo+LDD device exhibits a steeper conduction-band profile near the channel edges, indicating stronger electrostatic confinement and improved gate control.

The additional halo doping suppresses depletion-region encroachment from the source and drain, allowing the channel potential barrier to maintain a more abrupt and well-defined shape. As a result, DIBL, punch-through current, and short-channel effects are reduced while subthreshold behavior is improved.

LDD + HALO vs. Short-Channel : Transfer Characteristic Comparison

The transfer characteristics of the conventional short-channel MOSFET and the Halo+LDD device were compared under different drain biases. The conventional short-channel device exhibits larger threshold voltage variation and increased off-state leakage as drain voltage increases.

In contrast, the Halo+LDD structure maintains a steeper subthreshold slope and reduced drain-bias dependence. The improved electrostatic control suppresses DIBL and short-channel effects, resulting in lower leakage current and more stable threshold voltage behavior.

7. Gate-Induced Drain Leakage (GIDL)

GIDL Current Characteristics

Gate-Induced Drain Leakage (GIDL) was investigated by monitoring the drain, source, and substrate current components during a gate-voltage sweep. Under large negative gate bias, a strong electric field develops near the gate-drain overlap region, inducing band-to-band tunneling and generating leakage current even when the channel remains off.

The drain and source currents represent the resulting leakage path, while the substrate current indicates carrier generation inside the silicon body. The appearance of measurable current under negative gate bias confirms the onset of GIDL leakage caused by high-field tunneling rather than normal channel conduction.

8. SONOS Memory

SONOS Structure and Conduction Band Diagram

SONOS memory consists of an oxide-nitride-oxide stack, where charge is stored inside the silicon nitride layer. Unlike a floating-gate memory device, SONOS stores charge in localized trap states within the nitride layer.

The conduction-band profile illustrates the tunneling barrier between silicon and the nitride storage layer. Under programming bias, electrons tunnel through the thin oxide layer and become trapped inside the nitride layer.

Programming Operation

Increasing gate voltage increases electron trapping inside the nitride layer. Higher trapped charge produces a larger threshold voltage shift. The transfer curves shift toward higher threshold voltage as more charge is trapped, representing the programmed memory state.

Erase Operation

As the erase voltage becomes more negative, more trapped electrons are removed from the nitride layer, resulting in a lower threshold voltage and a stronger erase effect.

However, when the erase voltage becomes excessively large (e.g., -15 V), the strong electric field may induce unwanted charge injection or charge redistribution within the ONO stack, causing the threshold voltage to increase again.

9. CMOS Integration Theory

CMOS fabrication consists of FEOL processes for transistor formation and BEOL processes for interconnect fabrication. FEOL includes oxidation, photolithography, implantation, shallow trench isolation (STI), and well formation. BEOL includes metallization, chemical mechanical polishing (CMP), and interconnect formation.

Reflection

Through TCAD simulation, semiconductor concepts that were previously studied only theoretically became visually observable. In particular, short-channel effects, subthreshold conduction, DIBL, GIDL, and SONOS memory operation were better understood by examining electrical characteristics and physical distributions directly from simulation results.

Digital Tram Control System Design(FSM)

Completed

−

Fall 2025

Project Overview

This project involved designing a digital tram control system for an airport transportation setting using a finite state machine (FSM). The client requirement was to create a control system based on four given signals, while the overall FSM structure and implementation strategy were left open-ended. I designed a 6-state tram control system that determines motion and stopping behavior based on the relative progression of states rather than absolute floor position, making the design applicable regardless of the number of stops.

A key objective of my design was hardware minimization. Starting from the state diagram and present-state / next-state relationships, I repeatedly refined the state table and applied Karnaugh map (K-map) minimization to exploit symmetry and repeated logic patterns. By maximizing useful groupings and reducing Boolean expressions, I implemented the system with only 3 D flip-flops, 5 AND gates, 3 OR gates, and 2 NOT gates. The project was verified through digital simulation and received a full score.

My Experience / Project Concept

This project helped me strengthen my understanding of sequential logic design beyond basic FSM construction. I learned how to move from a conceptual control problem to a complete hardware-oriented implementation by building a state diagram, deriving a state table, simplifying logic with K-maps, and validating behavior through simulation. More importantly, I focused on designing the control logic as efficiently as possible rather than only making it function, which gave me stronger insight into digital hardware optimization.

Band-Pass Filter Design & Analysis

Completed

−

Nov 2024 - Dec 2024

Project Overview

This project focused on the design, theoretical analysis, and experimental verification of a band-pass filter (BPF). The goal was to understand how the circuit passes signals within a specific frequency range while attenuating signals outside that range, and to compare theoretical predictions with measured experimental behavior.

I derived the transfer function in the Laplace domain, analyzed cutoff frequencies, center frequency, and bandwidth, and used Bode plot concepts to interpret the circuit response in the frequency domain. Experimental measurements were then collected and compared with theoretical calculations. The results showed that the measured bandwidth matched the theoretical value closely, while the center frequency showed a larger deviation that was ultimately explained by component tolerances in the resistors and capacitors.

My Experience / Project Concept

This project helped me strengthen my understanding of frequency-domain circuit analysis and the relationship between mathematical modeling and real experimental behavior. I learned how to move from circuit configuration to transfer-function derivation, estimate filter characteristics using theoretical expressions, and interpret discrepancies between theory and measurement. In particular, I gained practical insight into how component tolerances influence the center frequency and overall filter response in real hardware.
